Q1

The mass of the moon is \(\frac{1}{144}\) times the mass of a planet and its diameter is \(\frac{1}{16}\) times the diameter of a planet. If the escape velocity on the planet is v, the escape velocity on the moon will be :

[JEE Main 2024, 31 Jan (Shift 2)]

a

\(\frac{v}{6}\)

b

\(\frac{v}{12}\)

c

\(\frac{v}{4}\)

d

\(\frac{v}{3}\)

🔒
Answer & explanation — PYQ Pass

Unlock this and thousands more solved PYQs.

Unlock · ₹149
✓ Correct answer: d)

\(\frac{v}{3}\)

Explanation

The formula for escape velocity is:

\[v = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}}\]

From the given problem, the ratios for the moon compared to the planet are:

Mass: Mm=Mp144

Radius: Rm=Rp16 (the ratio of radii is the same as the ratio of diameters)

Now, we can find the escape velocity on the moon (vm) by setting up a ratio:

\[\frac{v_m}{v_p} = \sqrt{\frac{M_m}{M_p} \times \frac{R_p}{R_m}}\]

Substitute the given values:

\[\frac{v_m}{v} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{144} \times 16}\] \[\frac{v_m}{v} = \sqrt{\frac{16}{144}}\] \[\frac{v_m}{v} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{9}}\] \[\frac{v_m}{v} = \frac{1}{3}\] \[v_m = \frac{v}{3}\]

Correct Option: D

Q18

Consider a star of mass \(m_2 \ kg\) revolving in a circular orbit around another star of mass \(m_1 \ kg\) with \(m_1 \gg m_2\). The heavier star slowly acquires mass from the lighter star at a constant rate of \(\gamma \ kg / s\). In this transfer process, there is no other loss of mass. If the separation between the centers of the stars is \(r\), then its relative rate of change \(\frac{1}{r} \frac{d r}{d t}\) (in \(s ^{-1}\) ) is given by:

[JEE Advanced 2025]

a

3γ2m2

b

2γm2

c

2γm1

d

3γ2m1

🔒
Answer & explanation — PYQ Pass

Unlock this and thousands more solved PYQs.

Unlock · ₹149
✓ Correct answer: b)

2γm2

Explanation

Conservation of Angular Momentum

For conservative mass transfer, the total orbital angular momentum (\(L\)) remains constant.

\[L = \mu \sqrt{GMr} = \frac{m_1 m_2}{\sqrt{m_1 + m_2}} \sqrt{Gr} = \text{constant}\]

(where total mass M=m1+m2 is constant).

Logarithmic Differentiation

Taking the natural logarithm of both sides to easily separate the variables:

\[\ln L = \ln m_1 + \ln m_2 + \frac{1}{2} \ln r + \text{constant}\]

Differentiating with respect to time \(t\):

\[0 = \frac{1}{m_1}\frac{dm_1}{dt} + \frac{1}{m_2}\frac{dm_2}{dt} + \frac{1}{2r}\frac{dr}{dt}\]

Apply Given Conditions

The heavier star gains mass (dm1dt=γ) and the lighter star loses mass (dm2dt=-γ). Substitute these rates:

\[0 = \frac{\gamma}{m_1} - \frac{\gamma}{m_2} + \frac{1}{2r}\frac{dr}{dt}\]

Since m1≫m2, the term γm1 is negligibly small compared to γm2:

\[0 \approx -\frac{\gamma}{m_2} + \frac{1}{2r}\frac{dr}{dt}\]

Final Rearrangement

\[\frac{1}{2r}\frac{dr}{dt} = \frac{\gamma}{m_2}\] \[\frac{1}{r}\frac{dr}{dt} = \frac{2\gamma}{m_2}\]

Correct Option: B

This equality is incorrect because gravitational potential energy is not defined as m g R in this context.
At infinite distance, gravitational potential energy is taken as zero.
U = 0
Hence the assertion is incorrect.
The reason stating potential energy is zero at infinity is correct.
